# 09  Product Launch Video Teardowns

## Fourteen launch films, the decisions that make them work, and what I'd test to improve them.

A launch film can be beautifully made and still leave someone unsure what the product does. We ran into that making Beam's first AI-edited video. The useful window-moving moment was buried beneath interface navigation. I went through fourteen other launch films to understand the editing decisions behind that problem, including approaches that deliberately have a different job.

# What fourteen launch films reveal

## 1\. The important moment deserves a bigger role

Granola turns scattered sources into a readable brief at about 0:21. Cursor Origin reaches Ready to Merge around 0:19. Claude Meetings reveals structured notes at roughly 0:47. In each, one visible state does more explanatory work than several preceding scenes. My first edit would be to identify that state and ask whether it has enough time and prominence, before touching transitions or soundtrack.

## 2\. The gap between a promise and its proof matters

CleanShot introduces editing controls well before the treated recording is visible. Simular spends time establishing the vision before the agent performs a recognizable task. Neither delay is automatically a mistake. The question is whether the intervening seconds give the eventual evidence more meaning. If they merely defer it, I'd test showing the payoff sooner.

## 3\. Real UI is persuasive only when people can read it

Linear's diffs have credibility because the film stays inside actual developer work. Firecrawl's cards make several capabilities easy to scan. Both invite the same test. Put the video at its real feed size, turn off the sound, and ask a new viewer to describe the action or result. An honest screen recording can still communicate poorly when the decisive details are tiny.

## 4\. A launch video can have more than one job

Vercel's twelve-second geometric announcement explains a conceptual relationship. Framer's keyboard film makes a physical object desirable. Notion's longer film has to connect several stages of software work. Judging all three by how fast real UI appears would miss the point. Decide whether the video needs to establish belief, show a finished result, teach a workflow, or create desire. Then judge it against that job.

## 5\. Reach tells us who encountered the post, not what they understood

The October 20 launch-video dataset reports 26.3 million public X post views for Cursor Origin. That's evidence of reach, not evidence of video completions or product comprehension. The full performance appendix separates public counts from editorial judgments. I won't use popularity as a substitute for evaluating the film itself.

## Find a starting point for your own launch

If several inputs become one result, start with Granola. If a user completes one concrete task, study Cursor Origin and Linear. For work happening live, watch Claude Meetings. For a connected system, study Notion and Simular. If the benefit is a visual comparison, look at CleanShot and OpenAI Ultrafast while keeping illustrative claims distinct from measured ones. If you're announcing several related capabilities, compare Firecrawl and Framer.

## How to use the index and teardowns

The film names in the index open their assessments below. Each assessment links to the original X film and includes a specific editorial revision I'd test. The timed observations come from a November 15, 2026 frame-sampling research archive and are approximate. We did not independently measure audience retention, conversions, or the soundtrack of every film. Suggested edits are hypotheses, not evidence that the original films failed.

Use the reviews as working examples. First choose a film with the same communication job as yours. Identify the one scene that earns its claim, inspect it at real distribution size, and design one alternate cut that changes what a new viewer understands. The index gets you to the reference. The teardown explains the decision worth borrowing.

# The eight primary teardowns

## 01  Granola Briefs  \|  Source material becomes a useful brief

At 0:00 the claim is that Granola can work while you sleep. A midnight clock appears at 0:03, a meeting enters at 0:07, and past meetings, web research, and email are established as sources by roughly 0:14. The personalized brief arrives at 0:21 and sits beside a call at 0:24. Runtime in the archive is 32.13 seconds.

The strongest decision is to earn the output. Seeing where the information comes from helps a new viewer believe the brief could be useful. The scene also turns an otherwise intangible AI process into something specific enough to recognize.

What I'd test next. Briefs are the payoff, yet a cold viewer waits about two-thirds of the runtime to see one. I'd preview a clear, cropped brief for a second or two in the opening, then return to the source-convergence sequence. The test is whether first-time viewers can describe the output after five seconds without losing confidence in its provenance.

**Make your version**

**Where it fits**  

Use this when the product turns scattered material into a usable output, such as research, meeting preparation, reporting, or customer insights.

**Production brief**  

Record three authentic input sources and the resulting artifact. A 25-second cut could preview the result (0–3s), establish the next meeting or decision (3–8s), show source convergence (8–17s), and hold the usable output in context (17–25s). Ask a new viewer which inputs were used and what became possible afterward.

## 02  CleanShot Studio Mode  \|  Make an editing result visible

This is the longest core film, approximately 80.79 seconds. It begins inside the product controls, introduces smart zoom around 0:18, demonstrates editing treatment on real UI around 0:36–0:43, and introduces export options around 0:52.

The film's strength is credibility. Zoom, cursor emphasis, and export treatment are visible behaviors in an actual editing environment. It makes the editor's craft legible. The tradeoff is that a new viewer sees controls well before the improved recording has delivered its full payoff.

What I'd test next. Start with a two-to-four-second matched raw-versus-enhanced recording of a genuinely hard-to-follow action. Then show only the controls responsible for that improvement. Keep playback speeds and framing comparable, and check whether viewers find the action easier to follow. A 30-second cut would test this without replacing the detailed product tour.

**Make your version**

**Where it fits**  

Use this when the product improves something the viewer already has, such as a recording, draft, screenshot, or presentation.

**Production brief**  

Capture an untreated and an improved version of exactly the same source. Make a 20-second comparison with the raw problem (0–4s), the real editing action (4–9s), a matched replay (9–16s), and the finished result (16–20s). Keep playback speed and content comparable. Ask whether the edited action is actually easier to follow.

[**Watch original film**](https://x.com/cleanshot/status/2094803447920967862)

## 03  Linear Diffs  \|  Trust the real interface

Linear opens inside its UI at 0:00. Reviews lead to code diffs by roughly 0:12. The AI rename suggestion appears around 0:33 with updated references following. The film takes approximately 51.95 seconds and stays close to a developer's actual task.

The clarity comes from showing recognizable work instead of generic claims about AI. Someone already comfortable with code review can follow the movement from review to action. A less technical viewer, or someone watching on a narrow phone, has to extract that meaning from small and dense UI.

What I'd test next. Isolate one rename and its consequence as the centerpiece, with the before and after states readable at feed size. Crop to the relevant code region while preserving enough context to establish a truthful workflow. I'd ask developers what changed, and ask non-developers what problem the software solved. Those are different comprehension tests.

**Make your version**

**Where it fits**  

Use this for technical work where the buyer needs to trust a real sequence of actions and see exactly what changed.

**Production brief**  

Record one authentic issue, diff, suggestion, and final state. Aim for a roughly 25-second guided sequence. Preserve the real ordering, show the point of action clearly, and zoom into changed lines without losing context. Check whether someone in the target role can name the operation and its consequence after one watch.

[**Watch original film**](https://x.com/linear/status/2060048202796040655)

## 04  Cursor Origin  \|  The successful state closes the loop

After an introduction, the product appears at about 0:03. The sequence includes New at 0:06, GitHub sync around 0:10, a repository and code states, and a Ready to Merge result at roughly 0:19. The film is approximately 30.90 seconds long.

Ready to Merge is the decisive frame because the intended audience immediately understands the value of arriving there. The edit avoids spending time on repeated navigation. Its weakness is mostly an opportunity. The recognizable success state appears only after several setup moments, so some viewers may leave before seeing what made the journey matter.

What I'd test next. Put a one-second preview of Ready to Merge in the opening, then go back to New and GitHub sync to show the real path. Compare the two edits on comprehension and meaningful click-through. Preserve the truthful sequence of the product's actions, even when shortening dead time.

**Make your version**

**Where it fits**  

Use this when the product turns setup into a familiar finished state, such as merge-ready, published, approved, deployed, or delivered.

**Production brief**  

Choose an authentic end state that the audience already values. Storyboard one glimpse of that state, then the shortest truthful path through three or four recorded steps, and finish by holding on the result. The key test is whether the viewer can say what was completed without being walked through the video again.

[**Watch original film**](https://x.com/cursor_ai/status/2089399057659596847)

## 05  Notion Ship OS  \|  One system, many connected steps

A real engineer introduces the workflow during approximately the first seven seconds. The film then moves from feedback triage to product documents, coding-agent work, status reporting, and a dashboard by around 0:54. Runtime is approximately 63.2 seconds.

A person supplies context that makes this more than an attractive montage of product screens. The story has a beginning and end that enterprise software often struggles to explain. There is still a lot of cognitive load. Each stage asks the viewer to absorb another feature while remembering how it relates to the original feedback.

What I'd test next. Add minimal stage labels and give one real artifact two seconds of breathing room before the next stage. I'd test an opening that names the finished outcome in plain language, then follows a single request from feedback to release. The success criterion is whether a new viewer can recount that connected sequence without a narrator filling in the gaps.

**Make your version**

**Where it fits**  

Use this when several features are valuable because they form one end-to-end system rather than a collection of independent functions.

**Production brief**  

Follow a single piece of work through input, triage, planning, execution, and delivery. Capture one actual artifact at each handoff. A 40–50-second version should label the stages, preserve their causal order, and give the final result enough time to register. Ask a viewer to describe what moved through the system and where it ended.

[**Watch original film**](https://x.com/NotionHQ/status/2075249002208227743)

## 06  OpenAI Ultrafast  \|  Parallel visual comparison

The roughly 45-second film splits the screen between Standard and Ultrafast rocket-building sequences. The different progress becomes visible early. While the slower rocket is still being assembled, the faster version has progressed toward launch and orbit.

The metaphor compresses the idea of speed into an instantly understood contrast. That is excellent for broad awareness. It does not, by itself, establish any measured performance advantage under real operating conditions. A viewer also leaves without seeing the product's actual interface or a concrete task.

What I'd test next. When the intended claim is empirical, pair the metaphor with a short, sourced comparison on the same task, with conditions and units explained. If it remains a brand film, keep the metaphor and avoid dressing illustrative timing as a verified benchmark. The editorial test is what numerical conclusion viewers think they saw.

**Make your version**

**Where it fits**  

Use this when the audience needs to understand a real contrast in time, quality, cost, or effort, or when a metaphor is the deliberate creative objective.

**Production brief**  

Choose literal proof or metaphor before production. For literal proof, record the same task under comparable conditions and disclose the measurement method. For metaphor, label the comparison as illustrative. A 15-second split-screen cut can establish the paired starting state, show divergence, and finish with one qualified takeaway. Ask what claim the viewer believes the visual proved.

[**Watch original film**](https://x.com/openai/status/2104993966043320759)

## 07  Claude Meetings  \|  Useful work happens during the meeting

The film starts inside a call, shows Claude entering the collaboration, builds whiteboard ideas around 0:14–0:28, sorts them by roughly 0:40, and reveals a structured notes document around 0:47–0:54. The research archive reports a runtime near 63 seconds.

Its strongest creative decision is to show intermediate work. The viewer sees the messy input, the agent's contribution, and a durable output. That sequence gives the final notes more credibility than a simple claim that AI can summarize meetings. The risk is a late result and a final document that can be too small to read in a feed.

What I'd test next. Hold the structured notes long enough to recognize decisions and open questions, using a readable crop. A brief opening preview of the result could also help unfamiliar viewers understand what the live collaboration is leading to. Keep the whiteboard sorting sequence, because it provides the most convincing evidence of real-time participation.

**Make your version**

**Where it fits**  

Use this when the product contributes while people or other systems are already working, particularly agents, collaboration, support, or live research.

**Production brief**  

Capture the human task, the agent's visible intermediate contribution, and the final artifact. A 30–35-second cut can move from activity to intervention to changed work to usable output. Don't imply a capability that was not actually captured. Ask a viewer to distinguish what the humans did, what the product did, and what they got at the end.

[**Watch original film**](https://x.com/claudeai/status/2120973994191094179)

## 08  Firecrawl Job Tools  \|  One outcome per product card

The approximately 27-second sequence begins with Job Tools, progresses through job recommendations, highlights an Ideal Match around 0:11–0:14, then introduces people and contextual information before a data-source and brand closer.

The card structure is easy to adapt when a product contains several related capabilities. It stays understandable without a speaker. The weak point is also typical of card-driven launches. Small details can disappear at mobile size, and a viewer may remember three attractive cards without understanding which job the product actually completes.

What I'd test next. Use one full-size matched job as the main proof, followed by the exact person and context that make acting on it easier. Let each card carry a new part of the same user task. The test is whether a viewer can name the result, the supporting information, and the next action after one muted watch.

**Make your version**

**Where it fits**  

Use this for a product with several closely related capabilities that help the same person finish one job.

**Production brief**  

Pick one representative user and one authentic result. Show the first useful card, then the additional person, evidence, or action that makes the result more valuable. Design each card to answer a different question while retaining one through-line. At mobile size, ask a viewer to name the progression, rather than recite feature labels.

[**Watch original film**](https://x.com/firecrawl/status/2120197091410493731)

# Six supporting films and useful counterexamples

## 09  Vercel Backends  \|  When abstraction is the point

The approximately 12-second film shows labeled frontend and backend geometry moving together. It contains no actual product screen. The motion is concise and memorable for viewers who already understand the architectural relationship.

I'd keep it as an example of a narrow communication job, an abstract announcement rather than a demonstration. If the aim were to persuade a developer to adopt a new workflow, I'd add a second cut showing a real request moving through the new system. The creative criticism depends on the intended job, not an automatic preference for UI footage.

[**Watch original film**](https://x.com/vercel/status/2110786348131258456)

## 10  Framer 3.0  \|  Turn the product's interface into its film

The roughly 75-second sequence uses the Framer canvas, publishing controls, a site preview, code interaction, CMS views, and a more abstract visual ending. It communicates a broad release through a series of related capabilities.

It demonstrates brand craft and a coherent visual language. The price is focus. Different viewers may remember different capabilities and miss the single most meaningful change. I'd try a version anchored by one complete before-and-after website result, then use the montage as a compact second act. Measure recall of the product change, not just reaction to the aesthetics.

[**Watch original film**](https://x.com/framer/status/2066939366568804508)

## 11  ElevenLabs Scribe v2  \|  A measurable claim becomes the visual

The 51.4-second film opens with abstract motion, introduces an accuracy chart around 0:11–0:17, then shows timestamp, keyterm, and entity-detection ideas. The archived video frame displays a 95.7% accuracy figure. That is a claim shown in the creative, not an independently verified performance result.

The chart is the clearest evidence-shaped scene. To make the announcement persuasive to a skeptical developer, I'd add the benchmark's task, dataset, comparator, and measurement definition, plus one short real transcription correction. That would connect the number to a job someone cares about. A numerical assertion should be auditable before a brand adopts it.

[**Watch original film**](https://x.com/ElevenLabs/status/2009626517521797288)

## 12  Framer F1 Keyboard  \|  Sell the object through craft

About 31.4 seconds of controlled lighting and macro keyboard photography create a premium physical-product reveal. The close-ups communicate materials, lighting, and the display key. This serves a different purpose from an app walkthrough.

For product desire, the visual detail is the evidence. I'd resist adding explanatory UI merely to satisfy a software-video formula. If the buyer's main question is why the keyboard is useful, however, one quick interaction with the display key could convert attention into understanding. That is the precise fork I'd test.

[**Watch original film**](https://x.com/framer/status/2059290932361699393)

## 13  Cursor for iOS  \|  Make a new surface feel natural

This approximately 74-second film intercuts a person in a casual setting with real phone UI. Review states, agent conversations, repository context, and notifications show what controlling coding agents from a phone might actually involve.

The environment makes the from-anywhere claim tangible. Its editing challenge is that phone screens become small inside social video. I'd build a shorter cut around one complete action, from notification to review to approval, with the human context used only where it clarifies why mobile matters. That keeps the claim grounded in actual behavior.

[**Watch original film**](https://x.com/cursor_ai/status/2071641103191998810)

## 14  Simular Box  \|  Vision needs a real task

The approximately 85-second launch starts with historical computers and a broader claim about autonomous machines, then moves through a founder explanation, a browser agent performing tasks around 0:38, and a time-and-cost comparison after roughly 1:04.

The ambition is memorable, and the eventual demonstration earns much of it. Yet the first authentic product action arrives relatively late. I'd test opening on the agent completing one recognizable task before moving into the vision. The side-by-side measurement should specify the same task, settings, and what the numbers measure. As with OpenAI's metaphor, I would never treat an on-screen comparison as independently validated performance.

# Our own production experiments

## Beam first and revised cuts

We made a Beam video using genuine screenshots and recordings. The first cut devoted too much attention to the picker and navigation. The stronger revision showed the decisive experience, a window from another Mac appearing and behaving as a local window. This was an editing-judgment failure more than a rendering failure.

The useful comparison includes the original source files, first render, specific feedback, revised render, and matched still frames. Both cuts should be linked when verified hosted URLs are available so readers can judge the change for themselves. Neither cut should be scored as a public launch or assigned an engagement number without the exact posted version and measurement window.

## Five HyperFrames slide animations

We also animated five original graphics from older presentations using Claude Cowork and HyperFrames. One made adoption stages concrete with stick figures, and another used dart throws to reveal results. Two graphics needed an extra prompt to repair motion or layout.

These are source-to-animation examples rather than external product launches. The professional evaluation is whether the motion helps someone follow the original relationships and sequence. The audit set should contain each original, rendered animation, correction prompt, and a still or GIF showing the decisive moment. It would be misleading to put their results into the launch-reach table.

# A reusable review scorecard

Score a film against the communication job it was supposed to do. Give each of the following a 1–5, but keep the exact frame and observed viewer response next to the score. Use 1 when the criterion is missing or misleading, 3 when it becomes clear with effort or prior knowledge, and 5 when it works unaided for the target viewer. A score is an editorial diagnostic, not an engagement result.

**Opening comprehension**  

After three seconds, can someone tell what situation this is about and why they should keep watching?

**Product payoff**  

Does the video visibly deliver the promised outcome? Identify its precise timestamp and how long it stays understandable on screen.

**Evidence integrity**  

Is the claim supported by authentic footage, correctly labeled comparisons, and a truthful sequence of product actions?

**Feed-size readability**  

Can someone see the decisive text or state at the actual viewing size, without pausing or zooming?

**Editing economy**  

Does each scene establish context, show evidence, or advance the payoff? What would be lost if it disappeared?

## The three-stop comprehension test

Show the film to someone who hasn't used the product. Pause after three seconds and ask what they think it is about. Pause at ten seconds and ask what they think the product does. At the final frame, ask what changed for the user and which shot convinced them. Write down the literal answers before giving context. Someone may like a video and still misunderstand the software.

To test a revision fairly, use comparable first-time viewers or randomly assign a first exposure to different cuts. Don't show the same person the original and then treat their understanding of the revision as an independent result. Record comprehension separately from liking, recall, click interest, and actual conversion.

## A worked example from Beam

Our intended payoff was a remote app window appearing on the current Mac like a normal local window. The first cut gave too much attention to choosing an app. We changed the emphasis so that the result had more screen time. The improvement is a stronger editorial hypothesis, not a measured comprehension lift. A proper next test would ask unfamiliar viewers to explain what Beam makes possible after each cut.

Keep the brief, first render, revision note, final render, and viewer reactions in one edit log. The next production cycle becomes easier because the criticism is attached to evidence and a specific change.

## What the length studies can tell us

Doomers AI's 2026 launch-video sample reports a median video length of 61 seconds. Among accounts with fewer than 10,000 followers, the 2–4 minute group had 546,000 median public X post views, compared with 151,000 for the 30–60 second group. The sample was drawn from a discovery directory that overrepresents launches people noticed, so these medians are not a causal rule for video length.

A separate Motion The Agency study of 1,750 B2B SaaS demo videos on YouTube found a 59-second median among uploads in 2026\. It classified 11% of videos shorter than 30 seconds in its top quartile of age-adjusted likes per view, against 26% at 30–60 seconds and 27% at 60–90 seconds. Those are like-rate quartile proportions within that study, not video retention rates and not directly comparable with the X counts.

These two studies push against a universal short-video rule. They do not prove that making a film longer increases reach. Audience, brand size, distribution, paid promotion, subject, and selection all affect the observed result. I would choose length based on the time needed to make the proof understandable, then test short distribution cuts separately.

## What performance data would make the analysis better

For every launch, I would capture the exact post timestamp, account followers when posted, public post views at 24 hours and 7 days, likes, reposts, replies, quotes, bookmarks, and profile or destination clicks when the owner shares them. The ratio of reposts or bookmarks to post views can be useful context, but it is not watch completion and shouldn't be interpreted as buyer intent without downstream evidence.

For a creator who owns the video, request 2-second video views, three-second holds if available, average watch time, completion at 25/50/75/100%, clicks, trial starts or registrations, and the exact date range. Ideally collect the first render and revised render under comparable distribution conditions. A high view count on a company launch says little about which editing decision caused it.

The original research log includes generic 3-second retention curves and strategy rankings. Those were synthesized estimates rather than observed retention for these exact films. They are useful hypotheses for the experiment plan but should not be printed as measured facts in the public video atlas.
