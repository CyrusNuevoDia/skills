# 07  The working kit

Keep the Video Brief, Shot Map and Edit Log together. They give the team a record of what the video is supposed to communicate, what source material supports it, and why the version changed.

## Start with five lines

Copy this before making a video. A short, concrete brief is easier to review than a long creative description.

**VIEWER AND SITUATION**

Who will watch, and what job are they trying to do?

**PROMISE**

What should they understand or believe after watching?

**MOMENT**

What scene would you fight to keep if the video had to be cut in half?

**EVIDENCE**

Which actual screen, recording, graphic or result supports the moment?

**NEXT ACTION AND QUALITY BAR**

What should they do afterward, and how will you know the video was clear?

### A real example from Beam

Viewer: someone using more than one Mac. Promise: a remote app behaves like a normal window here. Moment: that window arriving visibly on the current Mac. Evidence: real Beam screen recordings. Quality bar: someone unfamiliar with the product can explain what it makes possible.

## Make a Shot Map

Write down each scene before rendering and identify the real footage or screenshot behind it. That makes it easier to spot a missing step before AI fills the gap with something convincing but inaccurate.

| Scene | Viewer question | Actual source and check |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Opening** | **Why pay attention?** | **\[filename\] · \[recognizable job or result\]** |
| **Action** | **What happens?** | **\[filename\] · \[real interaction or change\]** |
| **Outcome** | **What is possible now?** | **\[filename\] · \[actual completed state\]** |
| **Close** | **What next?** | **\[verified CTA or intended takeaway\]** |

If a planned moment needs evidence you don't have, record the product or change the scene. Don't allow the renderer to fabricate a convincing but false interface.

## Keep one Edit Log

The log can be short. It should make the next revision a real creative decision.

**VIDEO AND VERSION**

Name the original and revised renders so they can be compared.

**INTENDED MEANING**

What should the audience understand?

FIRST-RENDER OBSERVATION

What did the video actually show? Include a timestamp.

**WHAT WAS MISSING OR WRONG**

Describe a specific failure in meaning, UI truth, pacing, framing or legibility.

**CHANGE MADE**

Which scene changed and why?

**WHAT THE NEW VIEWER UNDERSTOOD**

Record the reaction before explaining the product yourself.

## Prepare your source folder

Put real product screens and recordings in one folder. Add approved branding, any spoken footage, one sentence explaining the product's job, and links to two or three reference videos. For each reference, write which storytelling decision you admire. The links are for creative direction. Do not assume you can copy the source footage or a competitor's branded assets.

## Prompt 01  Inspect and plan

I'm making a launch video for \[PRODUCT\] aimed at \[AUDIENCE\]. The job someone needs to do is \[JOB\]. The result the viewer should see is \[OUTCOME\]. The video should last about \[DURATION\] and be delivered in \[FORMATS\].

Inspect the actual files in this folder and tell me what each one shows. Separate real product evidence from references and mockups. Do not invent UI, capabilities, measurements, customer quotes or source footage.

Propose three genuinely different approaches. For each, give me the first three seconds, a timed Shot Map, original filename for each scene, all on-screen copy, what the scene proves and what a new viewer could misunderstand. Tell me which one you recommend and why. Wait for my approval before rendering.

## Prompt 02  Render the approved plan

Build the approved Shot Map from the actual supplied assets. Preserve original speech meaning and product states. Keep the source material unchanged. Export the specified format, and give me the duration, dimensions, scene boundaries and a small set of frames including the product's most important moment.

Describe any deviation from the approved plan, missing material or unclear claim. Don't call the cut finished until it has been reviewed.

## Prompt 03  Review and revise

Watch the rendered video as someone who has never used the product. At each scene, say what the viewer can understand from the picture and which product claim that picture actually supports.

Check whether a phone-sized crop hides the important UI. Note inaccurate captions, changed speech meaning, invented screens and confusing jumps in the task. Recommend no more than three revisions, ranked by how much they'd improve understanding. Give exact timestamps and original source files. Wait for approval before changing the render.

## Prompt 04  Check before publishing

Compare the final video with the Video Brief, approved Shot Map, source files and Edit Log. Verify captions, format, actual product behavior, source claims, readability at the destination size, audience expectation and next action.

Return READY or NEEDS WORK, followed by exact issues with timestamps and what you were unable to verify. An empty issue list is not proof that the video works for its intended audience. Get an unfamiliar person to describe what they saw.

## Choose the right review for the source

**EXISTING FOOTAGE**

Verify that cuts preserve the speaker's meaning, including caveats. Ask for the proposed moments and timestamps before editing.

**PRODUCT UI**

Check that every shown state is real and that the important result is large enough to see. Avoid editing away essential steps until the workflow becomes misleading.

**DIAGRAMS AND GRAPHICS**

Compare the animated version with the original labels and relationships. Movement should help explain the diagram, and changes to its meaning need approval.

## Carry the learning into the next launch

Keep the final cut, the first render, the Video Brief, the Shot Map, and the Edit Log together. The next launch should start with what you learned about communicating the job, not a blank timeline.
