# casting.md

A skill for thinking through _who joins your venture, in what order, and to play what role_ — generalized so it works for a movement, a startup, a podcast, a fund, or any project where the first 10–25 people determine whether momentum compounds or stalls.

---

## The Core Move

> **Coalition-building is _casting_, not _collecting_.** A coalition isn't a list of impressive names. It's a play with named parts, and each role exists because the story cannot reach its ending without someone willing to play it. The job is not to maximize logos; it is to fill specific roles with specific people who will _show up_ when it matters.

Most founders treat coalition-building as a popularity tournament — accumulating endorsements, advisors, and famous names hoping that enough social proof will tilt outcomes. This produces _yearbook coalitions_: lots of names, no defense, no momentum. The more useful frame is **theatrical casting**: every role has a function in the plot, every actor has lines they will speak, and the play depends on whether the right people show up at the right time.

This skill is for thinking through the **first 10–25 people** of any new venture — the layer that determines whether the project develops legitimacy, capital, defenders, and momentum, or whether it stays a clever idea that no one helps build.

---

## Step 1 — Inventory the Warm Starting Position

Before listing anyone you _want_, list everyone you _already have_. The mistake almost every founder makes is sequencing cold outreach before mapping warm assets. The warm assets are usually stronger than they look and they almost always determine the order of everything that follows.

For each existing contact, write down:

- **Their actual standing** — not their title, but what they can credibly _do_ for you
- **Which layer of the coalition they fit** (see Step 2)
- **Who they can introduce you to** — the second-order graph is the real asset
- **Whether the relationship is warm enough to ask, or just warm enough to call**

> **The hidden insight**: the warm starting position usually contains 3–5 people who, used well, generate the next 20. The first conversations should _extract introductions and feedback_ — not ask for commitments.

---

## Step 2 — Identify the Layers This Particular Project Needs

Every venture needs a different mix. The wrong move is to import a generic template; the right move is to ask: _which forms of social, technical, and institutional capital does this specific project actually need to survive and win?_

The common layers — pick the ones this venture genuinely needs:

> **Air Cover Layer** — Public-reach allies who reframe attacks when they come. Needed whenever the project will provoke an incumbent who can fight back narratively. One well-placed tweet from this layer can be worth more than a million in legal fees.

> **Domain Legitimacy Layer** — People recognized within the project's home field as serious and credible. Needed whenever success depends on practitioners adopting it or experts validating it. _The single most underweighted layer in tech-led ventures aimed at non-tech domains._

> **Institutional Reach Layer** — People who can move things inside the organizations the project depends on (universities, government, NGOs, industry bodies). Needed whenever the project must be _adopted by_, _defended within_, or _not quietly killed by_ institutions. Usually invisible to outsiders; often decisive.

> **Capital Layer** — Patronage, institutional grants, strategic investment. Needed always but usually the _least scarce_ layer — don't optimize for it first.

> **Operational/Infrastructure Layer** — Strategic partners providing non-monetary capital: compute, data, distribution, technical platforms. These are commercial relationships dressed as ecosystem ones. Needed whenever the project's economics depend on someone else's infrastructure.

> **Legal/Defensive Layer** — Lawyers, advocates, or institutional shields who can speak to the project's defensibility _publicly_. Needed whenever the project sails closer to legal/regulatory wind than the average startup.

> **Public Voice Layer** — Writers, journalists, communicators who make the story legible at scale. Needed whenever success depends on cultural narrative shifting in your favor.

For _any_ venture, run the checklist: which of these does this project genuinely need? Which can be deferred? Which doesn't apply at all? _The answer shapes everything downstream._

> **Worked example — a podcast interviewing tech people with head and heart**: needs Public Voice (other podcasters, writers), Domain Legitimacy (a few well-respected interviewees who give the show its tone), Air Cover (early high-status guests who signal what kind of show this is). Probably doesn't need Legal Shield or Institutional Reach.

> **Worked example — a voluntary carbon market venture**: needs Domain Legitimacy (climate scientists, MRV experts), Institutional Reach (registries, standards bodies), Capital (climate-aligned investors), Legal Shield (carbon-market regulation is fraught), and Public Voice (climate journalists). Almost the full stack.

## Step 3 — Sequence into Waves

> **The compounding principle**: each "yes" should make the next "yes" easier. If you take a yes and don't extract both forms of value from it — _credibility update_ and _introduction generation_ — you're leaving compound interest on the table.

The standard sequence:

**Wave 1 — Warm Foundations (weeks 1–3).** The 3–5 people you already have. Job: _brief them_, get their feedback, get their permission to namedrop, and identify the next ring of intros. _No public commitments yet._

**Wave 2 — Anchors (weeks 3–8).** The handful of people whose names, once attached to the project, make every subsequent ask easier. Usually 2–3 names per layer — the legitimating figure in the domain, the air-cover figure, the legal anchor. _Their yes is what unlocks Waves 3–5._

**Wave 3 — Build-out (weeks 6–12).** Now you have a story. The project is real, the anchors are in, the framing is _"here are the people involved"_ rather than _"please consider supporting this."_ Inevitability attracts; uncertainty repels. Most of the coalition gets added in this wave.

**Wave 4 — Strategic & International (weeks 8–16).** Geographic balance, institutional partnerships, the diversification layer. The coalition becomes globally legible.

**Wave 5 — Outer Ring (ongoing).** Aware-and-supportive practitioners who'll use the project, talk about it, defend it organically. Not formally on the list, but real.

## The Six Principles

> **1. Composition matters more than count.** A coalition that's 70% practitioners and 30% capital reads correctly for a domain venture. The inverse reads as capital trying to capture the domain. _Defend the ratio._

> **2. Coalition is dry powder. Don't fire it early.** Public coalition launches are one-shot moments. Announce a star-studded list before there's a working product or clear narrative and you burn the social capital prematurely while giving opponents time to organize.

> **3. The punch you're preparing for shapes the coalition.** Every advisor is implicitly asked: _will you defend this in public when it gets attacked?_ Pick people who'll show up, not people who'll lend their name. The brand-cost calculation is real — some allies are powerful but polarizing in ways that cost you elsewhere.

> **4. Each yes used twice.** Once to update the pitch, once to generate the next intro. A yes that produces only credibility but no introductions is half-used.

> **5. Inevitability attracts; uncertainty repels.** By Wave 3 the framing should be _"here's what we're doing; here are the people involved"_ — not _"please consider supporting this idea."_ The same words land entirely differently depending on the framing.

> **6. Power and standing are not the same thing.** Capital allies give you power; domain allies give you standing. You need both, in roughly balanced public visibility. A power-only coalition reads as an extraction play; a standing-only coalition lacks the resources to withstand attack.

## Failure Modes

- **The yearbook coalition** — forty names, no scenes; looks impressive, defends nothing
- **Capital-first sequencing** — chasing money before legitimacy makes you look like you're laundering domain credibility through purchases
- **Premature public launch** — announcing a coalition before any of them have real skin in the game
- **Single-layer coalition** — heavy in one layer (usually tech patrons) without anchors in the layer that gives the project _standing_
- **Cold-pitching anchors** — the highest-value names rarely say yes to cold outreach; they say yes to inevitabilities. Sequence so they're approached when the project already feels real.
- **Listing without role** — never put someone on the list without knowing what scene they're in and what lines they'll speak

## How to Use This With Codex

When you bring me a new venture, give me:

1. **The shape of the project** — what is it, who is it for, who would oppose it
2. **The warm starting position** — every relevant contact you already have, with notes on standing and access
3. **Your read on which layers matter most** — and I'll push back if I disagree

Together we'll map the layers, sequence the waves, and produce a working list of the first 10–25 people. The output should be: _who, in what wave, via what intro, asking what._ Anything less specific than that is yearbook thinking.
