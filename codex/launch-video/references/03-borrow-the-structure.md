# 03  Borrow a storytelling structure

A useful swipefile stores decisions you can apply to your own product.

These eight launch videos solve different storytelling problems. Each example highlights one creative decision worth borrowing, along with a scene plan for trying something similar using your own product material and AI.

## 1\. Granola Briefs

### Show several inputs becoming one useful output

[**Watch Granola Briefs (32 seconds)**](https://x.com/meetgranola/status/2057100483219857868)

*Watch the source cards becoming a useful meeting brief around 0:17–0:21.*

The opening promises that Granola can work while you sleep. At roughly three seconds, a midnight clock makes that idea concrete. A calendar supplies an upcoming meeting. Then past meetings, web research, and email appear as sources.

Around 17 seconds, the film says it can produce a personalized brief. By about 21 seconds, the viewer sees the actual brief. A little later, that brief sits alongside a video call, showing where somebody would use it.

I like the sequence because the output feels connected to the source material. There's a clear path from information someone already has to something they can use before a meeting.

**The moment I'd borrow**

Make several messy or scattered inputs converge on one useful artifact. Show the artifact at a size where someone can recognize it.

**Where I'd use it**

An AI research assistant, analytics product, reporting feature, customer-insight tool, or anything that turns several information sources into one decision.

**What I'd need**

A real or representative calendar entry, two or three source examples, and an actual result from the product. Use fictionalized content if confidentiality is an issue, but don't invent the product's capability.

**A 25-second version I'd try**

| Time | Scene |
| :---- | :---- |
| 0–3s | Show the actual useful result and one short line explaining what it is |
| 3–8s | Show the upcoming task or trigger |
| 8–15s | Let three sources appear one at a time |
| 15–21s | Animate the sources into the output |
| 21–25s | Hold on the usable result in its real workflow |

**Direction I'd give an AI agent**

Inspect these source screenshots and the final output. Propose a five-scene video showing the sources becoming the result. Use the exact supplied labels and UI. The final output must be readable on a phone. Tell me which elements would be redrawn or animated, and wait for approval before rendering.

**What I'd check**

Can someone explain what inputs the product used and what it produced? If the scene looks magical but the result is hard to read, I'd fix that before adding another transition.

## 2\. CleanShot Studio Mode

### Let viewers see what the tool changes

[**Watch CleanShot Studio Mode (81 seconds)**](https://x.com/cleanshot/status/2094803447920967862)

*Notice the smart zoom around 0:18 and how export enters the story around 0:52.*

This is the longest example in the group, at about 81 seconds. The film starts inside Studio Mode. Around 18 seconds, it introduces smart zooms. Later, the viewer sees the effect of cursor movement and highlighting inside the recording. Around 52 seconds, it shifts to export options.

The film spends its time on visible editing behaviors. You can see why someone recording a product demo might want those controls.

I'd borrow the way the film makes individual editing controls understandable. It shows why a smart zoom, cursor emphasis or export choice might matter to someone trying to make an existing recording easier to watch.

**The moment I'd borrow**

Show an actual recording becoming easier to watch. A zoom, highlight, or reframing choice should make the viewer notice something they might otherwise miss.

**Where I'd use it**

Video editors, developer tools, presentation software, design products, recording tools, and products that improve an existing artifact.

**What I'd need**

A recording with a genuine problem, the product's real editing controls, and an exported version showing the change.

**A 20-second version I'd try**

| Time | Scene |
| :---- | :---- |
| 0–4s | A raw recording makes an important click hard to follow |
| 4–8s | Show the actual editing control applied |
| 8–15s | Replay the improved passage at a comparable speed |
| 15–20s | Show the result at normal phone size |

**Direction I'd give an AI agent**

Find one moment in the original recording where a viewer could lose track of the action. Propose a tighter crop, cursor treatment, or caption to fix it. Preserve the original task and timing. Render a labeled comparison using the same source recording for both versions.

**What I'd check**

Did the change help a stranger follow the task? If the original was already clear, extra movement may make it harder to watch.

## 3\. Linear Diffs

### Walk through one real task without leaving the product

[**Watch Linear Diffs (52 seconds)**](https://x.com/linear/status/2060048202796040655)

*Follow the review workflow through the AI rename suggestion near 0:33.*

Linear opens inside its product UI. At around 12 seconds, the viewer sees code diffs. The film progresses through review and code context, then shows an AI rename suggestion around 33 seconds and updated references shortly afterward.

The camera stays close to the real software. That makes the feature credible to somebody who already understands the job of reviewing code.

There's a tradeoff. Dense interfaces are easy to make beautiful and still difficult to read on a phone. A technical audience may recognize enough of the environment to follow the changes. Someone new to the workflow may need a stronger cue about the actual outcome.

**The moment I'd borrow**

One action that starts in a recognizable state, passes through the product, and ends in a changed state.

**Where I'd use it**

Work software that replaces a sequence of clicks, code review products, admin tools, workflow automation, and features with a specific task to demonstrate.

**What I'd need**

Accurate screen recordings of the task. Ideally, the recording should contain the real initial state, important interaction, and result.

**A 25-second version I'd try**

| Time | Scene |
| :---- | :---- |
| 0–4s | Start on the actual task or problem |
| 4–10s | Highlight the one place the user acts |
| 10–20s | Follow the action through the relevant screens |
| 20–25s | Hold on the completed result |

**Direction I'd give an AI agent**

Use only these authentic product recordings. Propose a 25-second guided walkthrough of the task from its starting state to its result. Identify the exact source frames and UI changes. Crop away anything the viewer doesn't need, but don't replace or invent UI states.

**What I'd check**

Does the viewer understand the action by the end? Can the important part of the interface still be read at the size it will appear in an X feed?

## 4\. Cursor Origin

### Make the path to a success state feel short

[**Watch Cursor Origin (31 seconds)**](https://x.com/cursor_ai/status/2089399057659596847)

*The key payoff is the Ready to Merge state, visible around 0:19.*

Cursor Origin takes roughly 31 seconds to move from an introduction into the product. By about six seconds, a user is starting something new. Around ten seconds, the video shows a GitHub sync. The sequence continues through code changes, and a Ready to Merge state appears around 19 seconds.

That last state is doing a lot of work. It's an outcome a developer can recognize immediately.

The creative choice I'd borrow is the economy of the sequence. We see just enough of the workflow to believe the result and imagine reaching it ourselves.

**The moment I'd borrow**

A familiar finished state, like a merge-ready change, completed report, published page, or working automation.

**Where I'd use it**

Coding tools, productivity apps, products that turn setup into finished work, and developer platforms.

**What I'd need**

Four or five authentic screens representing real progress, plus the exact result state. A product owner should verify that the sequence is possible.

**A 22-second version I'd try**

| Time | Scene |
| :---- | :---- |
| 0–3s | The task the user wants done |
| 3–7s | A single starting interaction |
| 7–15s | Two or three meaningful states, tightly edited |
| 15–20s | The finished state, clearly labeled |
| 20–22s | Product name |

**Direction I'd give an AI agent**

Find the shortest truthful path through these recorded UI states that ends in the approved success state. Create a timed scene plan. Remove repetitive navigation and idle waiting, but don't rearrange the workflow in a way that misrepresents what the product did.

**What I'd check**

Does the edited sequence still describe a real path through the product? A fast cut is useful until it starts implying steps happened that didn't.

## 5\. Notion Ship OS

### Explain a system by following work all the way through

[**Watch Notion Ship OS (63 seconds)**](https://x.com/NotionHQ/status/2075249002208227743)

*Watch how the human introduction sets up the connected product workflow.*

Notion uses a person on camera to establish the premise. An engineer introduces how a feature moves from customer feedback toward release. Then the film moves through triage, product documents, coding-agent work, status reporting, and dashboards.

At roughly 54 seconds, the viewer has seen several parts of the product acting as one system.

This example asks more of the viewer than a short feature announcement. The person opening the film supplies the context that makes the following product screens easier to understand.

**The moment I'd borrow**

A real human explaining the job, followed by an end-to-end workflow that earns the claim.

**Where I'd use it**

Enterprise software, products with multiple roles, agent platforms, team tools, and systems whose value emerges from how several capabilities work together.

**What I'd need**

A short real speaker clip or approved narration, genuine screen recordings of each major stage, and a truthful final output.

**A 45-second version I'd try**

| Time | Scene |
| :---- | :---- |
| 0–6s | Founder or product lead explains what work is being done |
| 6–15s | First task enters the system |
| 15–31s | Two or three connected steps happen |
| 31–40s | Final document, dashboard, or shipped result appears |
| 40–45s | Return briefly to the person or show the full outcome |

**Direction I'd give an AI agent**

Use this real spoken introduction and these recordings of the workflow. Propose an edit that keeps the explanation and the screen evidence aligned. Preserve the speaker's meaning. Show only the stages needed to make the end-to-end result understandable. Identify any missing step rather than inventing it.

**What I'd check**

Can a new viewer follow why the stages belong together? A complete system can become a confusing collection of features if the transitions don't explain what work is moving between them.

## 6\. OpenAI Ultrafast

### Make a difference visible through comparison

[**Watch OpenAI Ultrafast (45 seconds)**](https://x.com/openai/status/2104993966043320759)

*Compare the two animated rocket builds and what the visual difference makes memorable.*

The film uses a split-screen rocket-building metaphor, with one side labeled Standard and the other Ultrafast. Early in the video, the two sides start separating. The faster rocket is already on its way while the slower one is still being assembled. By the end, the difference is hard to miss.

The metaphor communicates speed with very little explanation.

I wouldn't confuse that visual impression with a measured benchmark. The animated sequence makes the claim vivid. Independent performance evidence would be needed to establish real-world speed under defined conditions.

**The moment I'd borrow**

Two comparable processes progressing simultaneously, so someone can see what changes.

**Where I'd use it**

Performance improvements, workflow reductions, product quality improvements, before-and-after comparisons, and technical product announcements that need a human-scale explanation.

**What I'd need**

Two genuinely comparable runs or two clearly fictionalized metaphor sequences. If the claim is numerical, use verified measurements.

**A 15-second version I'd try**

| Time | Scene |
| :---- | :---- |
| 0–2s | Two labeled versions start from the same state |
| 2–10s | Progress happens side by side |
| 10–13s | The different outcome becomes apparent |
| 13–15s | One carefully qualified takeaway |

**Direction I'd give an AI agent**

Make a side-by-side comparison using these two supplied runs. Match the starting event and keep playback speed accurate. Label conditions clearly. Add a clock only if the original timing is measured and comparable. If the tasks are different, explain that difference instead of presenting them as equivalent.

**What I'd check**

If the comparison is literal, are the conditions fair? If it's a metaphor, does it make a truthful proposition without pretending to be a measurement?

## 7\. Claude Meetings

### Show useful work happening before the meeting is over

[**Watch Claude Meetings**](https://x.com/claudeai/status/2120973994191094179)

*Follow the whiteboard work through to the structured notes around 0:47.*

The film opens inside a video call. Claude joins the conversation, then helps build a whiteboard with sticky notes. Later, the ideas are sorted into columns. By about 47 seconds, structured meeting notes appear, including decisions and open questions.

What makes this different from Granola is the sequence of work. Granola shows information being collected before an upcoming task. Claude Meetings shows the product participating while the human activity is still happening.

The final notes matter because the video has already shown the work that produced them.

**The moment I'd borrow**

A real-time activity becoming a useful artifact while people are still working.

**Where I'd use it**

AI coworkers, meeting products, design collaboration, customer support, research interviews, and software that helps a team coordinate.

**What I'd need**

A real or clearly staged meeting, source UI capture, an intermediate artifact, and the resulting notes, plan, document, or other output.

**A 35-second version I'd try**

| Time | Scene |
| :---- | :---- |
| 0–6s | People are already doing the task |
| 6–14s | The product enters and makes one helpful change |
| 14–25s | An intermediate artifact grows or changes |
| 25–32s | The finished useful result appears |
| 32–35s | Product name and concrete takeaway |

**Direction I'd give an AI agent**

Build a scene plan showing the product helping with a real ongoing task. Use source footage of the task, the actual intermediate artifact, and the final output. Make it obvious what happened during the activity and what was available afterward. Do not imply the agent completed work it didn't do.

**What I'd check**

Does the audience understand what the product contributed? A video showing people working together is easy to follow, but the incremental value of the agent needs to be visible.

## 8\. Firecrawl Job Tools

### Show several capabilities through compact product cards

[**Watch Firecrawl Job Tools (27 seconds)**](https://x.com/firecrawl/status/2120197091410493731)

*Notice how the Ideal Match badge gives one of the cards a specific result.*

Firecrawl uses a roughly 27-second sequence of product-style cards. The opening introduces Job Tools, then highlights a matching job. An Ideal Match badge makes the value of that card easier to recognize. The film proceeds to relevant people and context before ending on data-source logos.

Unlike a longer workflow narrative, it gives viewers a series of examples they can scan.

The trick is to make each card carry a distinct benefit. It's easy to produce a beautiful montage that says the same thing five times.

**The moment I'd borrow**

A highlighted, concrete result inside a product card. The viewer should know what changed when the card appeared.

**Where I'd use it**

Features built around lists, search results, recommendations, integrations, alerts, plugins, or toolkits with several closely related capabilities.

**What I'd need**

Real UI examples or accurately approved representative product cards, the correct result labels, relevant context, and a final brand card. Representative product cards can work well here, as long as the labels and examples accurately describe what the software does.

An 18-second version I'd try

| Time | Scene |
| :---- | :---- |
| 0–3s | One line introducing the job |
| 3–8s | First product card and highlighted result |
| 8–13s | Second card showing what additional context appears |
| 13–16s | Third capability or next action |
| 16–18s | Product name |

**Direction I'd give an AI agent**

Use the approved product cards in this folder to create an 18-second capabilities video. Show one distinct outcome per card. Keep the important data legible. Don't add capabilities or substitute fictional people and companies without making their example status clear.

**What I'd check**

Can the viewer name what each card demonstrates, or does the film feel like one long movement of floating rectangles?

## Don't copy the surface

Granola and Claude Meetings both produce useful artifacts, but one tells a preparation story and the other shows work happening during a meeting. Linear and Cursor both use real software footage, but give their workflows different amounts of time.

Choose a structure because its viewer question matches your promise. The typography, music, transitions and color are secondary to the job.

When you borrow a move, write down the real source footage you'd need to create your own version. If the source cannot support the story, don't copy the claim.

## Choose a structure for the viewer's question

Each example answers a different question. This quick selector can help you choose a structure before writing the Shot Map.

| Viewer question | Story structure | Example |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| What useful result do I get? | Inputs become output | Granola |
| How does the product work? | One guided UI task | Linear |
| How quickly can I reach the result? | Short path to success | Cursor |
| What improves an existing recording? | Visible editing control | CleanShot |
| How does the whole process connect? | End-to-end workflow | Notion |
| How different are the two outcomes? | Side-by-side comparison | OpenAI |
| What happens during actual work? | Live process to artifact | Claude |
| Which capabilities matter? | One outcome per card | Firecrawl |
