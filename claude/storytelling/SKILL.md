# storytelling.md

A skill for turning a plan, vision, or venture into a story that people can _project themselves into_ — so prospective allies, hires, investors, and customers walk into conversations already engaged rather than evaluating from scratch.

---

## The Core Move

> **Stories convert plans into trajectories. People can argue with claims; they can only choose whether to join trajectories.**

When you tell someone _"X is a good idea,"_ they evaluate the claim — is it true, is it valuable, does it apply to me. When you tell them _"here's how the world is changing, and here's what we're doing about it,"_ they evaluate the **trajectory** — does this match how I see things, do I want to be part of where this is going. The second register is much harder to argue with and much easier to _join_. This is why founders, movement leaders, and skilled communicators reach for narrative when stakes are high.

This skill assembles the **Pixar Story Spine**, the **two-key inciting-incident structure**, the **role-projection mechanism**, and a small set of craft principles that make the difference between a story that lands and a story that drifts.

---

## The Pixar Spine

The **Pixar Spine** is a six-beat narrative structure articulated by Kenn Adams and popularized by former Pixar storyboard artist Emma Coats. It is the _minimum viable story_ — short enough to fit in a paragraph each, structured enough to feel inevitable.

> _Once upon a time **. Every day **. One day **. Because of that **. Because of that **. Until finally **._

Each beat does a specific job:

> **Once upon a time** — Establishes the _good version_ of the world. The deal, the way things were, what was working. Without this, the conflict has nothing to push against.

> **Every day** — Establishes the _current dysfunction_. What's wrong, who suffers, who benefits from the dysfunction. The antagonist gets named here; the stakes get specified.

> **One day** — The _inciting incident_. The moment when something becomes possible that wasn't possible before. The reader should feel: _something has changed; the world won't stay the same._

> **Because of that × 2** — _Causal momentum._ Each "because of that" is a hinge — a consequence that makes the next thing inevitable. This is where the protagonist acts and the plot accelerates. Two beats is the minimum; you can extend to three for more complex arcs.

> **Until finally** — The resolution. _Where this all ends if it works._ Not a wish — a destination earned by the prior beats.

The genius of the spine isn't the structure itself; it's that it forces causal _momentum_. By the time the reader reaches "until finally," the conclusion feels earned, not asserted. Stories without this momentum drift; stories with it are inevitable.

## The Two-Key Inciting Incident

A common failure mode of the spine is a weak "one day." The inciting incident is supposed to mark a _threshold moment_ — but most ideas fail to identify what genuinely changed _and why now is the moment._

> **The two-key structure**: many genuine threshold moments require _two_ keys turning, not one. The first key is **long-standing possibility** (legal, scientific, economic, cultural). The second key is **recently-arrived capability** (technology, infrastructure, market readiness, political alignment). Either alone would not unlock the door. Both together do.

When you find yourself with a weak inciting incident, ask: _what was always-possible-but-impractical that just became practical?_ The second key is usually where your project's actual edge lives.

This structure also doubles as a credibility move: it answers the reader's implicit question _"why hasn't someone done this already?"_ by making explicit which key was missing and which one just turned.

> **Worked example — a carbon-market venture**: First key = the market has technically existed since the 1990s. Second key = satellite-based MRV (Monitoring, Reporting, Verification) just made fraud detectable at scale. Both together = the moment a credibility-first carbon market becomes buildable.

> **Worked example — a head-and-heart tech podcast**: First key = tech founders have always wanted deeper conversations than the standard interview circuit. Second key = the medium has matured enough that a slow, intimate format can find an audience without going through traditional gatekeepers. Both together = the moment for a different kind of show.

## The Role-Projection Move

The most underrated property of the Pixar Spine is that it has natural slots for _characters who arrive at specific moments in the plot_. The hero needs allies. Different allies show up at different beats. **You can use this structure to invite the reader into a role.**

The technique:

1. After the spine, list the **named roles** the story requires
2. Make the roles **archetypal but specific** — each one has a clear function and is distinct from the others
3. Leave them **deliberately empty** — name the role, describe what they do, do not name names
4. Frame the roles as _casting_, not as a request for endorsement

> **Why this works**: when someone reads "The Movement Elder," "The Public Voice," "The Builder," they unconsciously ask _which one am I?_ That's the moment the document does its work. They are no longer evaluating whether to support you — they are evaluating which role they want to play.

This is the same psychological move the Avengers franchise makes. You don't watch _Iron Man_ and think _"should I support the Avengers?"_ You think _"I'd be Hawkeye."_ **Identification beats persuasion every time.**

The roles should map roughly to the layers in [casting.md](./casting.md) — but reframed as characters in the story rather than slots in a coalition.

## Craft Principles

> **One concrete fact per beat.** Stories without specifics drift; specifics make a story feel like _the world as it actually is_ rather than a sales pitch. The dollar figure, the date, the named institution. But _one_ per beat — stat-heavy paragraphs break narrative rhythm and the reader hits a wall.

> **Story-forward beats stat-forward.** Statistics make arguments; stories make trajectories. When in doubt, embed the stat in a sentence whose primary register is narrative (_"matched and in places exceeded human experts"_) rather than analytical (_"achieved 96.96% accuracy with p < 0.001"_). Save the rigorous numbers for linked supporting documents.

> **Word budget discipline.** A working spine fits in 300–400 words. The full document — spine, roles, ask, links — fits in 600–900. The shorter it is, the more times someone reads it; the more times they read it, the more they can't unsee themselves in the story. _Length is the enemy of memorability._

> **Mythic register, sparingly used.** Phrases like _"and then the second key turned,"_ _"the moment when X actually becomes possible,"_ or _"for the first time in three centuries"_ signal threshold moments. Use them at hinges. Used everywhere they read as purple; used at the right moment they make the story feel _consequential_.

> **Lineage when relevant.** Movements have ancestors. Naming them precisely (Ginsparg, Eisen, Swartz; or for a different domain: Carnegie and Nightingale; or Ostrom and Hardin for governance ventures) signals that the project knows its place in a tradition rather than imagining itself unprecedented. Use this when the project descends from a real lineage; skip it when invoking ancestors would feel like name-dropping.

> **Name the antagonist precisely.** Sloppy enemy-naming costs allies. _"The publishers"_ is sloppy; _"the rent-extraction layer of academic publishing — Elsevier, Springer Nature, Wiley, Taylor & Francis, SAGE"_ is precise. _Precision attracts allies who agree with the specific framing; sloppiness alienates allies who would have agreed with a sharper one._

> **Hold tensions consciously.** Every real venture has internal contradictions (mission-vs-pricing, scientist-vs-tech-coded, open-vs-monetized, scale-vs-quality). Acknowledging them in the document does not weaken it; it strengthens it, because sophisticated readers who'd otherwise spot the tension and dismiss the project instead see it acknowledged and trust the writer more. _Don't pretend it's simple._

> **Match the call-to-action to the relationship stage.** Cold readers get _"let's get on a call."_ Warm readers get _"join us."_ Bought-in readers get _"sign here."_ Wrong escalation feels presumptuous; correct escalation feels natural.

## Cold vs. Post-Conversation Framing

> **The same story does different work depending on the order in which it appears in a relationship.**

A story sent _cold_ — before any prior interaction — has to do all the work of capturing attention, establishing legitimacy, making the case. It tends to read as ambitious and is easy to dismiss.

A story sent _after a conversation_ — once the relationship has begun — does different work entirely. It _crystallizes_ what was discussed, gives the reader something to point to and share, and converts a verbal hand-wave into a reference document.

> **Where possible, send the story _after_ the call, not before.** The cold version has to win attention; the post-conversation version has to lock in commitment. Different jobs, often confused.

A useful corollary: if you have a single document, optimize it for the _post-conversation_ job. The cold version can be a much shorter teaser (100–150 words) that previews the document and links to it.

## Failure Modes

- **Weak inciting incident** — _"one day, we realized..."_ or _"one day, AI got better."_ If your one-day beat is vague or generic, the spine collapses. Find the actual threshold moment.
- **Stat-stuffed body** — more than one statistic per beat reads as a pitch deck in prose form. Pull stats into supporting documents and let the spine breathe.
- **No roles** — a story without named slots gives the reader nothing to identify with. They read it, nod, and move on.
- **Mismatched register** — mythic register for a transactional ask, or analytical register for a movement story. Match the register to what you're actually trying to do.
- **Premature CTA** — closing with a pledge before earning it; closing with a soft "let's chat" when the reader is already bought in.
- **Too long** — every additional sentence dilutes the spine. If you can cut a clause without losing the meaning, _cut it_.
- **Generic antagonist** — "the system," "big tech," "the establishment." Vague enemies make weak stories. Name names.
